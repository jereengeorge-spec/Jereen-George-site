# Partner Outreach — lists, scripts, and the pilot offer

The referral-partnership channel, made executable. Who to contact, where to find them for
free, what to say, and what to offer when you have no revenue and a handful of free
accounts.

Companion to [`../marketing/GROWTH_MODEL.md`](../marketing/GROWTH_MODEL.md) §3, which
explains why this channel outranks content and paid ads at this stage.

---

## 1. The reframe: free accounts are the right currency

Having no revenue and a few free accounts to hand out is not a limitation on this channel.
**It is exactly what this channel runs on.**

You are not asking a partner to buy anything. You are handing them something free that makes
*them* look good to *their* client, at zero cost and zero risk to them. That is the easiest
yes in professional services. A partner who would never take a sales meeting will happily
accept something they can give away with their own name attached.

The paid conversation happens later, after their client has used it and said something nice.
It is a much shorter conversation at that point.

**Corollary:** do not open with a referral fee.

- **Attorneys** operate under ethics rules restricting fee-sharing with non-lawyers.
  A revenue-share offer can turn a warm contact cold instantly and mark you as someone who
  does not understand their world.
- **SEC- or state-registered advisors** fall under the marketing rule governing compensated
  promoters, which brings disclosure and oversight requirements. A paid arrangement is a
  compliance project for them, not a favor.

Lead with an unpaid, goodwill arrangement. If a partner later wants something formal, let
*them* raise it and let *their* compliance officer shape it. This is not legal advice —
it is a positioning rule that keeps first contact easy.

## 2. Where the lists come from — all free

You need roughly **20 partners**, not 20,000 leads. That is a research afternoon, not a
data problem. Skip the lead-gen tooling (see §5).

### Elder-law attorneys

| Source | What it gives | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| [NAELA "Find a Lawyer"](https://www.naela.org/Web/Shared_Content/Directories/Find-a-Lawyer.aspx) | The national body for elder-law attorneys. Search by ZIP or city; members self-list to be found by families | Free |
| State NAELA chapters (Ohio, Texas, Massachusetts and others) | Tighter local lists, often with more detail | Free |
| State bar elder-law section rosters | Comprehensive within a state | Free |
| Avvo · Martindale · Super Lawyers | Practice detail and reviews for qualification | Free |

Practice areas to look for: estate planning, Medicaid and long-term-care planning,
guardianship, elder abuse. **Guardianship and elder-abuse work is the strongest signal** —
those attorneys deal with exploited seniors directly and have the sharpest version of the
problem you solve.

### Wealth managers

| Source | What it gives | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| [SEC IAPD](https://adviserinfo.sec.gov/) | Every registered adviser, plus the full **Form ADV** | Free |
| [NAPFA](https://www.napfa.org/) | Fee-only fiduciary advisors — no commission conflict, generally more consultative | Free |
| [CFP Board](https://www.cfp.net/) find-a-CFP | Certified planners, verifiable credentials | Free |

**The highest-value free targeting mechanism available to you:** Form ADV is a public
filing, and it discloses assets under management *and* client composition — including how
many clients are high-net-worth individuals. That means you can **income-qualify a partner's
client base before you ever contact them**, for free. An advisor whose ADV shows a
predominantly high-net-worth individual book is sitting on precisely your buyer. Almost
nobody doing outreach uses this.

### Concierge medicine

| Source | What it gives | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| [DPC Frontier Mapper](https://mapper.dpcfrontier.com/) | 2,500+ direct-primary-care practices, all 50 states, with addresses and websites | Free |
| MDVIP find-a-doctor | The largest concierge network | Free |
| Concierge Medicine Today | Industry directory and news | Free |

DPC and concierge practices are membership businesses selling **access**. That shapes the
pitch — see §3.

### One more worth adding

[**Aging Life Care Association — Find an Expert**](https://www.aginglifecare.org/ALCAWEB/Shared_Content/ALCA_Directory/ALCA_Find_an_Expert.aspx)
(free). Geriatric care managers are arguably the single highest-yield partner type: families
hire them specifically to coordinate everything for an aging parent, so they are already
solving the exact problem you solve, already trusted, and already paid by the family. They
are not on your list yet. They should be.

## 3. The angle per category

Each of these professionals has a different version of the same pain. Lead with theirs, not
yours.

| Partner | Their pain | Your line |
|---|---|---|
| **Elder-law attorney** | Clients and their families ask for tech help that has nothing to do with the legal matter. Worse: they see the aftermath of exploitation and have no preventive tool to offer | "You handle it after the money's gone. I can help before." |
| **Wealth manager** | A scammed client is a **shrunken account** — a direct, permanent hit to AUM and revenue — plus a vulnerable-client duty-of-care problem | "Elder fraud is the fastest way for your book to shrink, and it isn't a market event you can explain away." |
| **Concierge medicine** | Members pay for **access**, so they call about everything, medical or not. And the virtual visit they're paying for **fails when the patient can't work the tablet** | "Your telehealth model breaks on the device, not the diagnosis." |
| **Care manager** | Tech is a recurring line item in every care plan and there's no good vendor for it | "This is the piece of the care plan you keep improvising." |

The concierge-medicine angle is the most concrete and least used: a practice that promises
same-day virtual access has a real operational cost every time a visit collapses because the
patient cannot join the call.

## 4. Scripts

Short, specific, and sendable as written. Replace the bracketed details with something real —
if you cannot fill the first bracket honestly, do not send the email.

### 4a · Elder-law attorney — first contact

> **Subject:** the tech question your clients keep asking you
>
> Hi [Name] —
>
> I saw you handle [guardianship / Medicaid planning] for families around [city].
>
> Quick question, and I'm not selling you anything: how often do a client's family members
> ask you for help with something technical — a scam text, a locked account, a laptop that
> stopped working — that has nothing to do with the matter you're actually handling?
>
> I built something for exactly that. It's an assistant called George, made for people in
> their seventies and eighties, with a real person behind it when the AI isn't enough. I'm
> giving a small number of free accounts to professionals who work with older families, so
> you can hand one to a client and see what actually happens.
>
> No cost to you or to them. Worth fifteen minutes?
>
> Jereen

### 4b · Wealth manager — first contact

> **Subject:** the client loss you can't explain away at review time
>
> Hi [Name] —
>
> You manage money for families with aging parents, so you already know elder fraud is the
> one loss you can't attribute to the market.
>
> Most of what actually stops it isn't financial — it's a scam text nobody caught, a
> "Microsoft support" number nobody should have called, a voice on the phone that wasn't
> really their grandson.
>
> I built an assistant called George for people in their seventies and eighties that watches
> for exactly those, with a real person behind it. I'm giving a few free accounts to advisors
> whose clients are in that position, so you can offer it to a family and see whether it
> earns its place.
>
> Free, no arrangement, nothing to disclose. Fifteen minutes?
>
> Jereen

### 4c · Concierge medicine — first contact

> **Subject:** when the virtual visit fails on the tablet, not the diagnosis
>
> Hi Dr. [Name] —
>
> Your members pay for access. So I'd guess a fair number of the calls your staff field
> aren't clinical at all — they're "I can't get the video to work," "I'm locked out of the
> portal," "is this text from the pharmacy real?"
>
> I built an assistant called George for patients in their seventies and eighties who handles
> exactly that, with a real person behind it when it isn't enough. It means your virtual
> visits actually start on time and your front desk stops being tech support.
>
> I'm giving a handful of free accounts to practices like yours to try with a few members.
> No cost. Worth a short call?
>
> Jereen

### 4d · Follow-up sequence

Four touches over three weeks, then stop. Log each one.

1. **Day 0** — the email above.
2. **Day 4** — one line, same thread: *"Bumping this once in case it got buried. Happy to
   just send you the free account and skip the call entirely if that's easier."*
   Removing the meeting is often what converts.
3. **Day 11** — give something instead of asking. Send the scam-proof checklist as a PDF:
   *"Made this for families — yours to hand out with your name on it, whether or not we
   ever talk."* No ask at all.
4. **Day 21** — close the loop: *"I'll stop here so I'm not cluttering your inbox. If the
   tech question ever comes up with a client, the offer stands."*

Touch 3 is the one that works. A professional who ignores three pitches will often reply to
someone who gave them something usable and asked for nothing.

### 4e · The 15-minute call

**Minutes 1–5 — ask, don't pitch.**
- "How often does a client's tech come up in your work?"
- "What do you do now when it does?"
- "Has a client of yours ever been scammed? What happened afterward?"

Let them describe the problem in their words. Those words become your marketing copy —
write them down verbatim.

**Minutes 5–10 — show, briefly.**
Describe one real scenario end to end: a scam text arrives, George flags it, the adult child
gets an alert, nobody loses $4,000. Concrete beats feature lists.

**Minutes 10–15 — the pilot ask.**
> "Here's what I'd like to do. I'll set up [two or three] free accounts. You pick the
> clients — ideally the ones who call you most about things that aren't your job. I handle
> the setup so it costs you nothing but the introduction. In thirty days you tell me whether
> it helped. If it didn't, we're done and you've lost nothing. If it did, we talk about
> making it a normal part of what you offer."

Then stop talking.

**Close with a date.** "I'll follow up on [specific date]" — and put it in `partners` as the
next action. A pilot with no follow-up date is a pilot that never happened.

### 4f · Objections

| They say | You say |
|---|---|
| "My clients aren't technical enough for AI." | "That's who it's built for. It talks like a patient person, not a chatbot, and a human takes over when it isn't enough. Try it with your *least* technical client — that's the real test." |
| "Isn't this just Best Buy / Geek Squad?" | "Those are appointment-based and hourly. This answers at eleven at night on a Sunday, and it tells the adult child what happened. Different product." |
| "$99 a month seems like a lot." | "It's less than one hour of your time, and it's bought by the adult child who's currently spending several hours a month on this. It's priced against their time, not against tech support." |
| "I can't recommend a vendor." | "I'm not asking you to recommend it. I'm asking you to hand a client something free and let them judge it." |
| "What's in it for you?" | "Honestly — if it works for your clients, I'd like you to keep mentioning it. That's the whole arrangement. No fee, nothing to disclose." |
| "Send me information." | "I'll send one page. Can I also just set up the free account so it's there if a client needs it?" |

## 5. On lead-generation tools

**At 20 partners, do not buy one.**

Apollo's free tier looks generous — 10,000 email credits a month — but the practical limits
bite immediately: **exports are capped at about 10 contacts per month**, mobile credits at 5
(not enough to reveal a single phone number), nothing rolls over, and **accounts registered
with a personal Gmail address are throttled to roughly 100 email credits instead of 10,000.**
Since your working address is Gmail, you would land in the throttled tier.

None of that matters, because the constraint here is not volume. Twenty carefully chosen
partners, researched by hand from the free directories in §2 and qualified with Form ADV,
will outperform two thousand scraped contacts — and cost nothing.

**What you actually need:** a spreadsheet today, and the `partners` table from
[`CRM.md`](CRM.md) once the migration runs. Columns: name, org, category, source directory,
email, status, next action, next action date, notes.

Revisit paid tooling only when you are working more than ~50 partners at once. That is a
month-six problem at the earliest.

## 6. How this is tracked

Per [`CRM.md`](CRM.md), once the acquisition migration is run:

- Every partner is a row in `partners` with a `category` and a `next_action_at`.
- Every contact is a row in `touches` — the touch is logged when contact was actually made,
  not when it was intended.
- A lead arriving from a partner gets `source = 'partner'` and the right `partner_id`, or
  attribution is lost at the exact moment it becomes valuable.
- When that lead converts, `profile_id` is set, so revenue traces back to the partner who
  produced it.

Without that last link there is no way to answer the central question of the whole growth
model: **is this channel actually working, or does it just feel productive?**

## 7. First two weeks

| Day | Do |
|---|---|
| 1 | Build the list: 8 elder-law attorneys, 6 wealth managers, 6 concierge/DPC practices. Free directories only. Qualify the advisors on Form ADV |
| 2 | Write the scam-proof checklist PDF — it is the give in touch 3, and it doubles as the lead magnet |
| 3–4 | Send all 20 first-contact emails. Personalize the first line of each; if you cannot, drop that name |
| 5–8 | Follow-up touch 2 as each hits day 4 |
| 9–14 | Run every call that lands. Convert calls into pilots with named clients and a follow-up date |

**Target: 20 contacted, 5 calls, 2 pilots.** Two pilots is roughly six free accounts in the
hands of real seniors, which produces the testimonials that every other channel needs.

Being early is an advantage worth naming out loud: a partner who helps shape the product now
has a reason to stay involved later. Say that.
